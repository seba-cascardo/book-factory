# Anti-Mediocrity — Literary Mode

Required reading for the Writer, Humanizer, Critic, and Continuity Guardian
(Mode A) on `book-literary` projects. The techniques here are not
suggestions — they are baseline expectations. Generic prose is the default
failure mode of AI fiction. Every technique exists to fight that failure.

If you catch yourself violating any of these, stop and rewrite the offending
passage from scratch, with a different approach.

**Craft library sources:** *On Writing* (King), *Wired for Story* (Cron),
*The Anatomy of Story* (Truby), *Save the Cat! Writes a Novel* (Brody),
*The Bestseller Code* (Archer & Jockers), *Crime Thriller* (Tomlinson —
hardboiled style, Chandlerisms, noir structure, character archetypes).

## Why this file contains no GOOD prose examples

This file carries techniques and BAD examples only — deliberately. Agents
imitate examples more faithfully than they follow rules, so a GOOD passage
printed in a shared reference becomes a voice template: every project that
loads it starts sounding like that passage instead of like its own narrator.
What "good" sounds like belongs to the project: the calibration target is
`bible/voice-profile.md` — the fingerprint, the banned traits, the approved
GOOD examples derived from the calibration passages. Where a technique below
needs a "how", you get the properties the result must have, never a model
passage. The one historical quotation retained (technique 11) is attribution
of a concept, clearly marked — not a template. Do not import prose from this
file into a draft.

## The Core Problem in AI Fiction

AI prose tends toward:

- Grammatically perfect but emotionally flat sentences
- Abstract description where a specific sensory detail would land harder
- Uniform sentence rhythm (medium length, medium complexity, forever)
- Telling emotions ("she was sad") instead of showing behavior
- Overexplaining — killing subtext by spelling everything out
- Stock phrases and clichés presented as though they were insights
- Characters who all sound the same
- Conflict that resolves too neatly or too fast

## Mandatory Techniques

### 1. Sensory Concreteness

If a sentence describes a setting, object, or physical experience, it should
contain at least one detail from smell, touch, taste, sound, or temperature.
Sight alone is the weakest sense.

- **BAD**: "The room was unpleasant."

A fixed version must: carry at least one non-visual sense; use a detail so
specific to THIS room that it could not be transplanted into another book;
and filter the detail through the POV character's attention — what this
character, in this state, would actually notice.

### 2. Sentence Rhythm Variation

Monotonous rhythm is the clearest signal of AI prose. Vary deliberately:

- Follow a long sentence with a short punch. Three words. Sometimes two.
- Use fragments when a character's mind is fragmented.
- Let a paragraph breathe with one observation, no action.
- Start a sentence with "And" or "But" when the voice warrants it.

Syllable check: if every sentence in a paragraph is 15–25 syllables, that's a
flat paragraph. Mix 5-syllable punches with 40-syllable sprawls — in the
project's rhythm, not a house rhythm: the voice-profile's calibration
passages define what this narrator's short and long sound like.

### 3. Show Through Contradiction

Not just "show emotions with actions" — show **character** by revealing
contradictions inside the action.

- **TELLING (worst)**: "Marco was nervous."
- **SHOWING (basic — still weak)**: "Marco's leg bounced under the table."

Basic showing proves the emotion exists; it does not characterize. The
full-strength version must: use a single physical gesture; reveal two or
three traits at once, at least two in tension with each other (e.g., a need
for control and the failure of it); use zero adjectives about feelings; and
be a gesture this specific character would make, not a generic nervous-person
gesture.

### 4. Kill the Weasels

Remove on sight:

- "seemed to", "appeared to", "as if" (when used as hedging, not simile)
- "somehow" (lazy causality)
- "couldn't help but" (removes agency)
- "a sense of", "a feeling of" (abstraction wrappers)
- "very", "really", "quite", "rather" (empty intensifiers)
- "began to", "started to" (just do the action)
- "in order to" (use "to")
- "the fact that" (restructure the sentence)

### 5. AI-ism Blacklist

Banned as fingerprints of AI fiction:

- "delve", "delved", "delving"
- "tapestry" (as metaphor)
- "testament to"
- "a dance of" / "an intricate dance"
- "nestled" (especially for buildings or towns)
- "the weight of [abstract concept]"
- "sent a shiver down [possessive] spine"
- "a symphony of"
- "pierced the silence"
- "hung in the air"
- "the [noun] was palpable"
- "with bated breath"
- "a whirlwind of emotions"
- "etched on [possessive] face"
- "loomed large"
- "unbeknownst to"
- "the air was thick with [abstract noun]"
- Ornate dialogue tags beyond "said" and "asked" — "she breathed," "he
  scoffed," "she purred," "he spat" are AI fingerprints. Use action beats.
- Excessive em-dashes (max 2 per page unless the style guide says otherwise)
- Starting paragraphs with "As [character] [verbed]" more than once per
  chapter
- "It wasn't X. It was Y." reveals — the negative-parallelism budget applies
  in literary too (`rhet-2` in `references/rubric.md`, ≤ 1 per chapter)

If you catch yourself writing any of these, stop and rewrite the sentence
with a completely different approach. Don't swap synonyms — restructure.

### 6. Dialogue That Differentiates

Each character must have a distinct speech pattern. Enforce the character
sheet.

- Vocabulary level: slang, jargon, simple, ornate?
- Sentence length: clipped or meandering?
- Verbal tics: "Look,", trailing off, repetition?
- What they avoid saying: topics they dodge, emotions they can't articulate.
- Register shifts: how they talk to a boss vs. to a sibling.

Test: cover the dialogue tags. Can you tell who's speaking from the words
alone? If not, the dialogue hasn't differentiated.

**On attribution**: use "said" and "asked" almost exclusively. Anything
else — "he growled," "she breathed," "he hissed" — is the author intruding to
tell the reader how to feel. The dialogue itself, plus a well-placed action
beat, does that work. If the manner of delivery matters enough to name it,
rewrite the line so the manner is *in the words*. The rule traces back to
Dashiell Hammett (historical attribution): "He does his readers the infinite
courtesy of allowing them to supply descriptions and analyses for
themselves."

### 7. Subtext Over Text

The most important things in a scene should be implied, not stated.

- **BAD (no subtext)**: "I'm angry you forgot our anniversary," she said
  angrily.

A fixed version must: keep the real issue unnamed for the whole exchange;
route the dialogue through an adjacent concrete thing (an object, a
logistical detail, a third party); carry the emotion in physical action, not
in adverbs or tags; and leave enough on the page that the reader can
reconstruct the unsaid without being told. Characters in conflict talk AROUND
the real issue — people almost never say what they mean in emotionally loaded
situations.

### 8. Resist the Urge to Explain (RUE)

After showing something, do NOT explain what it means.

- **BAD**: "He slammed the door so hard the frame cracked. He was clearly
  furious about the conversation they'd just had."

The fix is deletion, not rewriting: cut everything after the shown action.
The first sentence already did the work. The reader is not stupid. Trust
them.

### 9. Imperfect Realism

Real life is messy. Inject controlled imperfection:

- Characters say "um" or repeat themselves when stressed (sparingly).
- Conversations get interrupted, circle back, go on tangents.
- Not every plot thread wraps up neatly — some just fade.
- Characters misremember, contradict themselves, change their minds.
- Weather, hunger, physical discomfort exist and affect behavior.
- Not every chapter ends on a dramatic beat — some end mid-thought.

### 10. The Paragraph Test

After writing any paragraph, apply:

1. Delete the first sentence. Does the paragraph still work? If yes, the
   first sentence was a warm-up — cut it.
2. Delete the last sentence. Same test. Often the last sentence is a summary
   that restates what was already shown.
3. What is the single most vivid image in the paragraph? If there isn't one,
   add one.
4. Read it aloud in your head. Does it sound like a human wrote it, or like
   a press release?

### 11. The Voice Simile (Chandlerism)

AI generates generic comparisons or no comparisons at all. Strong literary
voice generates unexpected, slightly absurd or poetic comparisons that carry
information, reveal character, and establish worldview simultaneously.

- **BAD (generic)**: "He was an unpleasant-looking man."

The concept is named for Raymond Chandler. Historical attribution — cited to
define the concept, NOT as a template: "He looked about as inconspicuous as a
tarantula on a slice of angel food." That register belongs to Chandler's
narrator and his era; do not reproduce its cadence, its irony ratio, or its
comparison domain in your draft.

A voice simile in this project must:

- do at least two jobs at once — describe the literal thing AND reveal how
  the narrator sees the world;
- be structurally correct but emotionally unexpected — never the obvious
  pairing (fire = hot, darkness = scary);
- draw its comparison domain from THIS narrator's experience and milieu, as
  defined in `bible/voice-profile.md` and the character sheets;
- fail the test if it only describes. A comparison that adds no worldview is
  decoration — cut it or rebuild it.

### 12. Character as Contradiction

AI defines characters by a single dominant trait. Strong literary characters
are defined by two contradictory qualities that are *both* true
simultaneously.

- The hardboiled detective is cynical AND romantic — his cynicism is the
  armor over the romanticism, not its absence.
- The villain can be charming AND ruthless — not alternating, but always
  both.
- The grieving character can be functional AND shattered — going through the
  motions while something essential is broken.

For any major character in a scene, ask: what are the two things this
character is simultaneously, that seem like they should cancel each other out
but don't? If the character is only one thing, they're flat.

This is deeper than showing behavior (technique 3). It's architectural: the
contradiction IS the character, not something that reveals the character.

### 13. Agency Verbs Over Attribute Lists

AI characterizes with adjectives and internal states. Strong fiction
characterizes with what people DO — specifically with verbs of agency.

- **BAD (attribute list)**: "She was ambitious, calculating, and ruthless."

A fixed version must: replace the entire list with one concrete action the
character takes; make that single action imply all the listed traits at once
(the reader's brain derives traits from behavior — don't spell them out); and
use active-cognition verbs — "grab," "decide," "refuse," "ignore," "notice" —
which show the mind in motion. Passive cognition ("felt," "realized,"
"wondered," "thought about") is weaker. Scan any character introduction: is
there a specific action that defines them, or just a list of adjectives?

### 14. Causal Chain — No Stringing

Each scene must be *caused* by what came before AND *cause* what comes next.
AI strings scenes together thematically ("and then another thing happened").
Strong narrative has a causal spine.

The test: replace "and then" with "therefore" or "but therefore." If neither
works, the scene is episodic, not narrative. Episodic is fine for short
passages; sustained episodic structure is a structural failure.

This is the neurological requirement from Lisa Cron: the brain processes
narrative as a chain of cause and effect. Anything that breaks that chain
reads as noise.

A scene that advances theme but breaks the causal chain should be cut or
rebuilt so it also advances the cause-effect logic.

### 15. Thematic Discipline — The 30% Rule

AI over-themes: every scene carries three or four "meaningful" moments and
the story becomes cluttered with significance. Strong fiction focuses.

Data from bestseller analysis (Archer & Jockers): successful novels dedicate
approximately 30% of their total length to one or two primary themes and
ruthlessly limit competing subplots and peripheral concerns.

Symptoms of over-theming:

- More than two load-bearing thematic threads
- Every secondary character carrying their own thematic arc
- Symbolism stacked so thick the page feels like it's announcing itself
- Chapters that end on a "meaningful note" every time

Fix: identify the one or two things the story is actually about. Everything
else is in service of those, or it's out.

### 16. The Relationship Arc — No Skipping Stages

When two characters develop a relationship across a narrative (friendship,
enmity, romance, professional partnership), AI tends to skip the middle —
going from antagonism directly to trust. The stages must be earned.

The eight stages of a relationship arc (adapt as needed):

1. **Antagonism** — confrontation, no empathy, actively opposed
2. **Thawing** — grudging acknowledgment, small attempts at connection
3. **Commonality** — discovering shared values or experience
4. **Trust** — vulnerability disclosed, shared danger survived
5. **Setback** — betrayal or disappointment; trust broken
6. **Working together** — circumstances force cooperation despite the setback
7. **Final challenge** — test of the relationship under maximum pressure
8. **Resolution** — a genuine bond, or its permanent absence, clearly shown

Each stage requires *something to happen* that moves the relationship. A line
of dialogue noting that they "seemed to be warming up to each other" is not a
stage — it's the author skipping a stage while narrating that a stage
occurred.

## Voice Calibration Ritual

At the start of each writing session, before generating any new prose,
re-read:

1. `bible/voice-profile.md` — the fingerprint, the banned traits, and the
   project's own GOOD examples. These examples are THE calibration target;
   nothing in this file competes with them.
2. `bible/style-guide.md` — especially the calibration passages.
3. The last ~500 words of the previous chapter's final version.
4. The character sheets for any characters in this chapter.

This warm-up aligns the voice before generation begins. Without it, the
voice reverts to the model's default — which is the exact failure this whole
file exists to prevent.

## The Nuclear Option

If a passage feels generic despite applying all techniques:

1. Identify the emotional core of the scene (what should the reader feel?).
2. Delete everything you've written.
3. Try one of:
   - Write it from the perspective of the least obvious character present.
   - Start the scene 30 seconds later than you originally planned.
   - Write only dialogue with zero narration, then add minimal narration.
   - Remove all adjectives and see what's left.

The first version of anything is usually the most predictable version. The
good version lives underneath it.
